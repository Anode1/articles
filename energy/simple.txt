PLAIN WORDS: THE PROBLEM AND WHAT I PROPOSE

For when someone asks "so what are you actually saying?" — four lengths,
shortest first. Use whichever fits the room.


=== ONE SENTENCE ===

Provinces should sell electricity to data centres at a published price
instead of handing out permissions, and should sell a cheaper kind that
can be switched off a few dozen hours a year.


=== THIRTY SECONDS ===

Right now, if you want to plug a big data centre into a Canadian grid,
you don't buy power. You apply for permission. BC holds a contest.
Alberta set a cap and it's already full. Quebec wants to double the
price. Ontario passed a law letting a minister decide, and hasn't
written the rules.

That's the wrong shape. When a monopoly has more buyers than supply, it
either names a price or picks winners. All four picked winners.

What I propose: post two prices. One for firm power that's always on.
One, cheaper, for power the grid can cut off when it's tight — about
85 hours a year, under one percent of the time. Then connect anyone who
pays. That's it. No new agency, no national agreement, no one has to
change their mind about climate policy.

The reason this works is that AI training doesn't need to run every
minute. It's batch work. It already stops and restarts constantly on
its own, because the hardware breaks. A data centre would happily agree
to pause 85 hours a year in exchange for connecting in 2027 instead of
2031. Nobody in Canada is allowed to make that trade.


=== TWO MINUTES ===

THE PROBLEM

Everyone wants to build data centres. The power equipment to serve them
is sold out — the biggest turbine maker is booking deliveries for 2031.
So nothing anyone builds changes the next three years. The only thing
still in play is who gets to use the grid we already have, and on what
terms.

Canada answered that question badly, in four provinces at once. Buying
electricity for a large load stopped being a purchase and became an
application. BC releases a fixed amount and runs a competition for it.
Alberta set an interim cap of 1.2 GW against 16 GW of requests, and it's
full. Quebec wants to roughly double the price by regulatory decree.
Ontario passed a law letting a minister approve connections and never
wrote the criteria.

None of that is indecision. Each one is a firm, fast decision that the
seller of electricity will choose the buyer.

Two things follow.

First, the value goes to the wrong people. In November a company sold
its 180 MW Alberta allocation — just the permission, no plant, no
building — for $18 million. AESO handed that permission out for free.
So the scarcity is worth real money; it just never reaches the grid that
created the scarcity. A price would have collected that.

Second, developers don't wait. They build their own gas or diesel behind
the meter and connect to nothing. That's worse on every measure: dirtier,
bound by no reliability standard, no connection agreement, no benefit to
anyone else on the system. And it needs nobody's approval, which is what
makes it the likely outcome.

And it's not free for households either. A grid that can't sell
flexibility has to build for the peak instead. Ordinary customers end up
paying for peaking capacity that a handful of large customers would
gladly have been paid to go without.

WHAT I PROPOSE

Act like a seller, not a gatekeeper.

1. Post a price for firm service and a price for interruptible service,
   and connect anyone who pays. The interruptible offer is the whole
   thing: accept deep curtailment, connect in 2027 instead of 2031.
   It's an offer, not a levy. It taxes nobody and subsidises nobody.

2. Put the behaviour in the contract, not in a rulebook written later.
   How the load rides through a fault, when it curtails — agree that
   before the concrete is poured, not retroactively onto buildings
   already financed.

3. Take deposits and require site control. That's how a seller tells a
   real buyer from someone holding a free option, and it thins the queue
   without anyone judging whose project is worthier.

4. Drop the rest — quantity caps, per-project ceilings, scoring on
   criteria the applicant can't see, ministerial discretion with no
   published basis. Each is a substitute for a price and worse than the
   price it replaces.

5. Don't wait for the other three provinces. One province posting real
   terms takes the business, and the other three have to answer it the
   following year. Alberta is the obvious one: it has the queue, it has
   surplus it's currently exporting, and its market was built for exactly
   this kind of price signal.

WHY THE TRADE IS REAL

The customer being turned away is the most flexible large load any grid
has ever been offered. Model training is a batch job built to be
interrupted — Meta's biggest run hit 419 hardware failures in 54 days
and saves its state every four minutes as a matter of course. Asking it
to pause is asking it to do something it already does, unprompted,
hundreds of times a run.

And the grid barely needs it. Duke's estimate is that the largest US
balancing areas could absorb 76 GW of new load at a quarter of a percent
curtailment — about 85 hours a year. Alberta's actual problem is winter
evenings in December and January. A handful of hours. A handful of hours
is exactly what a price can solve and a quota cannot.

WHAT I'M NOT SAYING

Not that curtailment is cheap — once the servers are bought, an idle
hour throws away about eight times more in standing capital than it
saves in power. That's the point: this buyer values uptime enormously
and the seller needs relief rarely. Those are exactly the two parties
who should be able to strike a deal.

Not that the reliability concern is fake. AESO was right that connecting
every large load *firm* would impair reliability. But an interruptible
connection isn't a claim on the winter peak, which is precisely why
there's room in grids that look full. The constraint is a reason to sell
the interruptible kind, not to refuse it.

Not that this is about climate policy. The federal rules don't bite
until 2035, the carbon price was cut this year, and AESO explained its
cap by citing request volume and reliability, not Ottawa. The constraint
is that the seller is a monopoly, and monopolies facing excess demand
ration instead of transacting.


=== IF THEY PUSH BACK IN ONE LINE ===

"Isn't the queue proof of a crisis?"
  A queue is an upper bound on interest, not demand. Positions are free
  and non-binding; developers file in several provinces to shop terms.
  Generation queues convert at 13-20%.

"Isn't Alberta short of power?"
  Not today — prices fell 30% in 2025 to an eight-year low and it
  exported six times what it imported. It's short on winter evenings.
  That's hours, not megawatts.

"What if these customers leave and ratepayers eat the cost?"
  Then charge for that in the contract — deposits, exit terms, minimum
  take. That's a seller's problem with a seller's solution, and it's
  cheaper than refusing the sale.

"Doesn't this need Ottawa / a national strategy?"
  No. It needs one province to post a number.
