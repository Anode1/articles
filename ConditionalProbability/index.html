Abstract

This paper is for the clinician, epidemiologist, or patient who meets one calculation in four notations and reads only one of them. The four are the probability tree, Bayes' theorem of probability theory, the 2×2 contingency table of epidemiology, and the odds and likelihood-ratio form of evidence-based medicine. The mathematics is Bayes' theorem on one worked example (1% prevalence, 80% sensitivity, 9.6% false-positive rate, positive predictive value 7.8%). The natural-frequency literature since Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) has shown that counts are the form people get right, and evidence-based medicine derives its diagnostic statistics from one 2×2 table. This paper writes the correspondence between all four notations out row by row, and adds four things of its own. First, the mapping table, with the odds form as an invertible change of coordinates and with the absolute and relative risk reduction (ARR, RRR) of trial reporting placed in it. ARR and RRR are computed within a trial arm, as sensitivity is computed within the sick; the predictive value is computed within those who tested positive. Second, a five-step Diagnostic Interpretation Procedure, whose first four steps are the natural-frequency method and whose fifth runs three checks, one per documented failure mode: arithmetic error, base-rate neglect, and a relative figure taken for an absolute one. Third, a spreadsheet form of the procedure for sequences of tests, with a sum check after every step. On the running example, two conditionally independent positive results raise the posterior from 7.8% to 41.2%, and a second test that repeats half of the first test's false positives raises it only to 13.2%. Fourth, the argument that medical and epidemiology curricula should teach all four notations through the mapping, not the contingency table alone. The fifth step of the procedure has not been tested on students or clinicians.
