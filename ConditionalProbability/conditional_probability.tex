\begin{abstract}
\noindent This paper is for the clinician, epidemiologist, or patient who meets one calculation in four notations and reads only one of them. The four are the probability tree, Bayes' theorem of probability theory, the $2\times 2$ contingency table of epidemiology, and the odds and likelihood-ratio form of evidence-based medicine. The mathematics is Bayes' theorem on one worked example ($1\%$ prevalence, $80\%$ sensitivity, $9.6\%$ false-positive rate, positive predictive value $7.8\%$). The natural-frequency literature since Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) has shown that counts are the form people get right, and evidence-based medicine derives its diagnostic statistics from one $2\times 2$ table. This paper writes the correspondence between all four notations out row by row, and adds four things of its own. First, the mapping table, with the odds form as an invertible change of coordinates and with the absolute and relative risk reduction (ARR, RRR) of trial reporting placed in it. ARR and RRR are computed within a trial arm, as sensitivity is computed within the sick; the predictive value is computed within those who tested positive. Second, a five-step Diagnostic Interpretation Procedure, whose first four steps are the natural-frequency method and whose fifth runs three checks, one per documented failure mode: arithmetic error, base-rate neglect, and a relative figure taken for an absolute one. Third, a spreadsheet form of the procedure for sequences of tests, with a sum check after every step. On the running example, two conditionally independent positive results raise the posterior from $7.8\%$ to $41.2\%$, and a second test that repeats half of the first test's false positives raises it only to $13.2\%$. Fourth, the argument that medical and epidemiology curricula should teach all four notations through the mapping, not the contingency table alone. The fifth step of the procedure has not been tested on students or clinicians.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

On the running example of Section~\ref{sec:problem}, a positive result on a screening test at $1\%$ prevalence is a false alarm in 92 cases of 100. This follows from Bayes' theorem, and it has been misread since it was first measured. Casscells, Schoenberger, and Graboys (1978) \cite{casscells1978} gave 60 physicians and students a problem with a prevalence of 1 in 1{,}000 and a $5\%$ false-positive rate, sensitivity taken as perfect. The answer is under $2\%$; 11 gave it, and 27 answered $95\%$. Base-rate neglect, the name for that error, was stated for clinical diagnosis by Meehl and Rosen (1955) \cite{meehl1955} and studied by Kahneman and Tversky (1973, 1974) \cite{kahneman1973, tversky1974}. Eddy (1982) \cite{eddy1982} catalogued the same errors, among them the confusion of the probability of disease given a positive test, $\Pr(D^+ \mid T^+)$, with the probability of a positive test given disease, $\Pr(T^+ \mid D^+)$, later named the inverse fallacy \cite{koehler1996, villejoubert2002}. An informal Russian version of the present treatment appeared online in 2012 \cite{lj2012}.

Part of the persistence is notational. Each of the four notations of the abstract is read by one profession and by few others, and the calculation is one. Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) \cite{gigerenzer1995} restated Bayesian problems in natural frequencies, absolute counts in place of probabilities, and correct answers among students rose from $16\%$ to $46\%$. Hoffrage and Gigerenzer (1998) \cite{hoffrage1998} found the same in physicians; later work drew the counts as trees and $2\times 2$ tables and measured both \cite{binder2015, binder2018, binder2021, kunzelmann2022}, and a meta-analysis puts the pooled gain at about 20 percentage points \cite{mcdowell2017}. Gigerenzer et al.\ (2007) \cite{gigerenzer2007} treat absolute and relative risk reduction (ARR, RRR) and the number needed to treat beside natural frequencies. Evidence-based medicine derives sensitivity, specificity, the predictive values, the likelihood ratios, and the nomogram from one $2\times 2$ table \cite{jaeschke1994}, and Binder et al.\ \cite{binder2015} set the tree and the table with the same counts side by side. None of them tabulates the correspondence across all four notations, or places ARR and RRR in the same table. Section~\ref{sec:iso} gives the mapping, Section~\ref{sec:proc} the procedure, Section~\ref{sec:sheet} its spreadsheet form, Section~\ref{sec:disc} the curriculum argument.

\section{The Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

One worked example runs through the paper. It is the mammography problem of Eddy \cite{eddy1982} in the form used by Gigerenzer and Hoffrage \cite{gigerenzer1995}, kept so that the results below can be compared with that literature. Let
\begin{align*}
  \Pr(D^+) &= 0.01 && \text{prevalence in the screened population,}\\
  \Pr(T^+ \mid D^+) &= 0.80 && \text{sensitivity,}\\
  \Pr(T^+ \mid D^-) &= 0.096 && \text{false-positive rate,}\\
  \Pr(T^- \mid D^-) &= 0.904 && \text{specificity, } 1 - 0.096.
\end{align*}
Here $D^+$ is the event that the person has the disease and $D^-$ that they do not, written like the two test results $T^+$ and $T^-$. $\Pr(A \mid B)$ is the probability of $A$ given that $B$ has happened, and $\Pr(A \text{ and } B)$ the probability that both happen; the two are linked by $\Pr(A \text{ and } B) = \Pr(A \mid B)\,\Pr(B)$. A probability of the form $\Pr(A \text{ and } B)$ is called joint. A marginal probability is a sum of joint probabilities over one of the two events, such as $\Pr(T^+) = \Pr(T^+ \text{ and } D^+) + \Pr(T^+ \text{ and } D^-)$. Conditioning is dividing a joint probability by a marginal one. With more events, $\Pr(A \mid B, C)$ is the probability of $A$ given both $B$ and $C$, and $\Pr(A, B, C)$ the probability that all three happen. The odds of an event are its probability over the probability of its complement, $\Pr(A) / (1 - \Pr(A))$, and a probability is recovered from its odds as $\text{odds} / (1 + \text{odds})$.

The question is the positive predictive value $\mathrm{PPV} = \Pr(D^+ \mid T^+)$, the probability that a person with a positive result has the disease. The clinic also uses ``false-positive rate'' for $1 - \mathrm{PPV}$, the share of positive results that are wrong; here it always means $1 - \text{specificity}$. Intuition puts it near the sensitivity, $80\%$. It is $7.8\%$. We derive that value once per notation, then exhibit the correspondence.

\begin{equation*}

\end{equation*}

\subsection{Bayes' theorem}
\label{sec:bayes}

The same calculation, algebraically:\footnote{For the general theory rather than the diagnostic special case: Jaynes \cite{jaynes2003}, Sivia and Skilling \cite{sivia2006}, MacKay \cite{mackay2003}; Cox \cite{cox1946} for probability as an extension of logic; Gnedenko \cite{gnedenko1976} and Ventsel \cite{ventsel1965} for the Russian university and engineering traditions, both in English translation.}
\begin{align*}
  \Pr(D^+ \mid T^+) &= \frac{\Pr(T^+ \mid D^+)\,\Pr(D^+)}{\Pr(T^+ \mid D^+)\,\Pr(D^+) + \Pr(T^+ \mid D^-)\,\Pr(D^-)} \notag \\
  &= \frac{0.80 \times 0.01}{0.80 \times 0.01 + 0.096 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.008}{0.10304} \approx 7.8\%.
\end{align*}
The numerator is the true-positive leaf. The denominator is the marginal probability of a positive test, the sum of the two positive leaves.

\subsection{Odds and likelihood ratios}
\label{sec:odds}

Evidence-based medicine writes the same calculation in odds, defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem}. The prior odds are the odds of disease before the test, $0.01/0.99$. The likelihood ratio of a positive result, $\mathrm{LR}^+$, is the ratio of the two positive edges of the tree, and it multiplies the prior odds; $\mathrm{LR}^-$ is the same ratio for a negative result:
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{LR}^+ &= \frac{\Pr(T^+ \mid D^+)}{\Pr(T^+ \mid D^-)} = \frac{0.80}{0.096} \approx 8.33, \\
  \mathrm{LR}^- &= \frac{\Pr(T^- \mid D^+)}{\Pr(T^- \mid D^-)} = \frac{0.20}{0.904} \approx 0.221, \\
  \text{posterior odds} &= \text{prior odds} \times \mathrm{LR}^+ = \frac{0.01}{0.99} \times 8.33 \approx 0.0842, \\
  \mathrm{PPV} &= \frac{\text{posterior odds}}{1 + \text{posterior odds}} = \frac{0.0842}{1 + 0.0842} \approx 7.8\%.
\end{align*}
The Fagan nomogram \cite{fagan1975}, a chart carried at the bedside, is this multiplication drawn on a ruler (Figure~\ref{fig:fagan}): a straight line from the prior probability through the likelihood ratio meets the posterior. The posterior odds are the ratio of the two positive leaves, $0.008 / 0.09504$, and of the two cells in the $T^+$ column, $800 / 9{,}504$.

The left axis places each probability at the height $-\log(\text{odds})$, the right axis at $+\log(\text{odds})$; a straight line between the two points crosses the middle axis at the average of their heights, $\tfrac{1}{2}(\log(\text{posterior odds}) - \log(\text{prior odds})) = \tfrac{1}{2}\log \mathrm{LR}$. The middle axis is drawn at half scale, each likelihood ratio at $\tfrac{1}{2}\log \mathrm{LR}$, so the line meets it at the ratio. That is the whole construction: the three points of one multiplication lie on one line. On the running example, in base-10 logarithms, the prior is at $1.996$, the posterior at $-1.075$, and their average $0.460$ is $\tfrac{1}{2}\log_{10} 8.33$. The minus sign on the prior axis is why its scale runs upward from $99.9\%$ to $0.1\%$ and the posterior scale the other way, and the logarithm is why $1\%$ and $99\%$ sit at equal distances from $50\%$ on both.

The chart works in any direction: from a pretest probability and the posterior one would need to act, it reads off the likelihood ratio a test would have to have. It carries no counts, so it does the multiplication of step 4 and none of the checks of step 5 in Section~\ref{sec:proc}.

\subsection{ARR and RRR}
\label{sec:arr}

Absolute and relative risk reduction compare two groups of a trial, called its arms: the treatment arm, which received the treatment, and the control arm, which did not. That is two populations rather than two states of one population, so they need a second example. Take a five-year primary-prevention trial with $10{,}000$ people per arm, $200$ events $E$ in the control arm and $50$ in the treatment arm; the number needed to treat, NNT, is the reciprocal of ARR:
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{ARR} &= \Pr(E \mid \text{control}) - \Pr(E \mid \text{treatment}) = 0.020 - 0.005 = 0.015 = 1.5\% \text{ over five years}, \\
  \mathrm{RRR} &= \frac{\Pr(E \mid \text{control}) - \Pr(E \mid \text{treatment})}{\Pr(E \mid \text{control})} = \frac{0.015}{0.020} = 75\%, \qquad
  \mathrm{NNT} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{ARR}} \approx 67.
\end{align*}
ARR and RRR are two readings of one $2\times 2$ table, arm against outcome, both axes relabelled from the diagnostic table; each risk is a cell over its row total. The same data give a $75\%$ RRR and a $1.5\%$ ARR; the two answer different questions, and a report that carries only the relative one leaves the reader no way back to the absolute scale. An ARR carries its time horizon or it means nothing, and NNT is its reciprocal over the same horizon.

The last four rows of the table, the odds rows, are a change of coordinates. Write $p$ for the prevalence, $s$ for the sensitivity, and $f$ for the false-positive rate; these three numbers fix the whole table. The odds form describes the same table by three other numbers: the prior odds, $\mathrm{LR}^+$, and $\mathrm{LR}^-$. Each triple is computed from the other:
\begin{align*}
  \text{prior odds} &= \frac{p}{1-p}, & \mathrm{LR}^+ &= \frac{s}{f}, & \mathrm{LR}^- &= \frac{1-s}{1-f}; \\
  p &= \frac{\text{odds}}{1 + \text{odds}}, & s &= \frac{\mathrm{LR}^+ (1 - \mathrm{LR}^-)}{\mathrm{LR}^+ - \mathrm{LR}^-}, & f &= \frac{1 - \mathrm{LR}^-}{\mathrm{LR}^+ - \mathrm{LR}^-}.
\end{align*}
The first line is one-to-one for $s \neq f$; equality would be a test whose positive rate is the same with and without the disease, which carries no information. Its image is the set of triples with positive prior odds, both ratios positive, and $(\mathrm{LR}^+ - 1)(\mathrm{LR}^- - 1) < 0$, one ratio above $1$ and the other below, and on that set the second line inverts it and returns $s$ and $f$ between $0$ and $1$. A triple outside it, $\mathrm{LR}^+ = 2$ with $\mathrm{LR}^- = 3$ say, comes from no test: it would give $f = 2$. On the running example the inverse recovers the inputs:
\begin{align*}
  1 - \mathrm{LR}^- &= 0.7788, & \mathrm{LR}^+ - \mathrm{LR}^- &= 8.1121, \\
  s &= \frac{8.3333 \times 0.7788}{8.1121} = 0.80, & f &= \frac{0.7788}{8.1121} = 0.096.
\end{align*}
Between those two sets the map is a bijection, so nothing in the table is available in one notation and missing in another; that is the sense in which the four notations carry one object. A practitioner who holds Table~\ref{tab:map} can read a paper written in any of the four.

\section{A Diagnostic Interpretation Procedure}
\label{sec:proc}

The errors of Sections~\ref{sec:problem} and~\ref{sec:arr} are errors of procedure: a number taken from the wrong row, or a relative figure taken for an absolute one. The procedure below is for a clinician, epidemiologist, journalist, or patient facing a test result or a trial finding. It takes three numbers and a population size $N$: the pretest probability $p$, the sensitivity $s$, and the false-positive rate $f$ of the test. The false-positive rate is $1 - \text{specificity}$, not the share of positive results that are wrong. On the running example $p = 0.01$, $s = 0.80$, $f = 0.096$. Checks (a) and (b) apply to a test result; check (c) applies to a trial figure. Steps 1--4 are the natural-frequency method \cite{gigerenzer1995, hoffrage1998}; step 5 is this paper's addition.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=0.4em]
  \item \textbf{Find the pretest probability.} For a screening programme, take the prevalence of screen-detectable disease in the invited age band. Programme reports print a detection rate per round, which is that prevalence times the programme's sensitivity, so divide the detection rate by the sensitivity to recover the prevalence. For a patient with a presentation, take the pretest probability for that presentation from a validated prediction rule or a local audit, not the population prevalence. For a trial, take the control-arm event rate from the primary outcome table of the primary publication. If the source gives only a relative measure and no base rate, stop: the figure cannot be interpreted.
  \item \textbf{Convert every rate to counts in a fixed population.} Multiply $N$ by $p$ for the number with the disease; the rest do not have it. Multiply the number with the disease by $s$ for the sick who test positive; the rest of them test negative. Multiply the number without the disease by $f$ for the well who test positive; the rest of them test negative. Round each count. Choose $N$ so that the smallest of the four counts is at least 10; on the running example it is the sick who test negative, $10{,}000 \times 0.01 \times 0.20 = 20$.
  \item \textbf{Fill the $2\times 2$ table of counts.} The four counts of step 2 are the four cells: disease and positive, disease and negative, no disease and positive, no disease and negative. Add the row totals and the column totals.
  \item \textbf{Compute the answer from the counts, not from a relative summary.} For a positive result, PPV is the disease-and-positive cell over the positive column total. For a negative result, the probability of disease is the disease-and-negative cell over the negative column total. The same two numbers come by the odds route, posterior odds $=$ prior odds $\times \mathrm{LR}$, with prior odds $= p / (1 - p)$, $\mathrm{LR}^+ = s / f$ for a positive result, $\mathrm{LR}^- = (1 - s) / (1 - f)$ for a negative one, and the probability $= \text{odds} / (1 + \text{odds})$. The two routes agree. ARR is the control-arm event count over the control-arm size minus the treatment-arm event count over the treatment-arm size. RRR is ARR over the control-arm rate; NNT is $1/\mathrm{ARR}$.
  \item \textbf{Run three checks before accepting the answer.}
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em,itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \emph{Sum check.} The four cells sum to $N$, within one unit per rounded cell; a larger gap is an arithmetic error.
    \item \emph{Bracket check.} For any test with $\mathrm{LR}^+ \geq 1$, the probability after a result lies in a bracket whose ends need only the pretest probability and the likelihood ratio:\footnote{With $p$, $s$, and $f$ as in Section~\ref{sec:iso}: $\Pr(T^+) = p\,s + (1-p)\,f$ lies between $f$ and $s$, and when $s \geq f$ the lower of the two is $f$, so $\mathrm{PPV} = p\,s / \Pr(T^+)$ lies between $p\,s / s = p$ and $p\,s / f = p \cdot \mathrm{LR}^+$. The same argument on $\Pr(T^-)$ gives the negative bracket. Dividing numerator and denominator of $\mathrm{PPV} = p\,s / (p\,s + (1-p)\,f)$ by $f$ gives the exact form in the text.}
    \begin{gather*}
      \text{prevalence} \;\leq\; \mathrm{PPV} \;\leq\; \text{prevalence} \times \mathrm{LR}^+, \\
      \text{prevalence} \times \mathrm{LR}^- \;\leq\; \Pr(D^+ \mid T^-) \;\leq\; \text{prevalence}.
    \end{gather*}
    A positive result cannot leave the probability of disease below the pretest probability, and cannot multiply it by more than $\mathrm{LR}^+$; a negative result cannot leave it above the pretest probability. A figure outside its bracket is wrong, at the stated pretest probability: a PPV quoted for a different population, a referral clinic against a screening programme, has a different bracket.

    The lower end of the positive bracket catches a PPV computed over the wrong total: $800 / 100{,}000 = 0.8\%$ is below the prevalence of $1\%$. The upper end catches base-rate neglect, a PPV reported near the sensitivity, only when that end is itself below the sensitivity, which happens when the prevalence is below the false-positive rate (since $p \cdot s / f < s$ exactly when $p < f$). On the running example $1\% < 9.6\%$ and the upper end is $8.3\%$. At higher prevalence the upper end says nothing: at $p = 0.10$, $s = 0.90$, $f = 0.09$ it is $1.00$, and a reported $90\%$ passes although the PPV is $52.6\%$. Whenever the upper end reaches $1$, or whenever the bracket is wider than the question, compute the exact value, which costs one more operation: $\mathrm{PPV} = p \cdot \mathrm{LR}^+ / (p \cdot \mathrm{LR}^+ + 1 - p)$, and compare it with the count route of step 4.
    \item \emph{Absolute-against-relative check.} Never accept an RRR without the ARR and NNT beside it, over a stated horizon. The ratio $\mathrm{RRR}/\mathrm{ARR}$ equals one over the control-arm event rate: a large ratio is a low baseline risk, which is when the relative figure misleads on its own.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

Pretest probability, here the prevalence, $1\%$; $N = 10{,}000$. Then $100$ have the disease, of whom $80$ test positive and $20$ negative; $9{,}900$ do not, of whom $950$ test positive ($9{,}900 \times 0.096 = 950.4$, rounded) and $8{,}950$ negative. Step 3 fills Table~\ref{tab:run}.

Step 4:
\begin{equation*}
  \mathrm{PPV} = \frac{80}{80 + 950} = \frac{80}{1{,}030} \approx 7.8\%.
\end{equation*}
Checks: (a) $80 + 20 + 950 + 8{,}950 = 10{,}000$; (b) $1\% \leq 7.8\% \leq 0.01 \times 8.33 = 8.3\%$; (c) no relative measure was reported. Accepted. For a negative result, step 4 reads the disease-and-negative cell over the negative column total, $20 / 8{,}970 = 0.22\%$; by the odds route, $0.0101 \times 0.221 = 0.00223$, the same. Section~\ref{sec:table} used $N = 100{,}000$ and got $9{,}504$ false positives; the rounding here changes no ratio at the precision shown. A reader can run the three checks without an external authority.

\section{The Spreadsheet for Sequences of Tests}
\label{sec:sheet}

When there is more than one test (screen then confirmation, longitudinal monitoring, tests of different modality), the procedure of Section~\ref{sec:proc} takes a flat spreadsheet form. It is the two-test tree of Binder et al.\ \cite{binder2018} flattened into rows, with a correlated case added. Each row is one path of outcomes; each column from left to right is one step; the rightmost column is the joint probability of the path, rounded to five places. After every step the rightmost column must sum to $1$. That is the sum check, step 5(a) of Section~\ref{sec:proc}, at that depth.

Suppose the test of Section~\ref{sec:problem}, prevalence $1\%$, sensitivity $0.80$, false-positive rate $0.096$, single-test PPV $7.8\%$, is applied to the same patient twice, with results $T_1$ and $T_2$. Assume for the moment that the two outcomes are conditionally independent given disease status. That means: among the sick, the second result does not depend on the first, and the same among the well. The assumption is a teaching device: two runs of one test on one specimen or platform share error sources (specimen quality, calibration drift, operator, biological confounders) and are positively correlated. When it fails, the Test 2 column holds $\Pr(T_2 \mid T_1, D^+)$ and $\Pr(T_2 \mid T_1, D^-)$ in place of $\Pr(T_2 \mid D^+)$ and $\Pr(T_2 \mid D^-)$ (Table~\ref{tab:corr} below). Those are the probabilities of the second result given both the first result and the disease status. The multiplier applied in step 2 then depends on the row's Test 1 entry as well as on its disease status. The shape of the spreadsheet does not change. The assumption is most defensible when the tests differ in analyte or mechanism, a serological screen followed by a nucleic-acid confirmation, say, so that the error sources are distinct.

After $n$ tests the spreadsheet has $2^{n+1}$ rows, two disease states times $2^n$ outcome paths.

\paragraph{Reading off a posterior.} Filter the rows on the observed outcomes; sum the matching joints for $D^+$; divide by the sum of all matching joints. After two positive results the matching rows are 1 and 5:
\begin{equation*}
  \Pr(D^+ \mid T_1^+ \text{ and } T_2^+) = \frac{0.00640}{0.00640 + 0.00912} = \frac{0.00640}{0.01552} \approx 41.2\%.
\end{equation*}
In this example the second result moves the posterior from $7.8\%$ to $41.2\%$. Whether that justifies the next, more invasive, diagnostic step depends on a threshold set per disease from the costs of the two errors, a missed case and a needless intervention; this paper sets none.

\paragraph{The pruned form.} The clinic observes one path, so at each step it needs only two rows: the posterior of the previous step as the prior of the next. After $T_1^+$ the two rows are rows 1 and 3 of the step 1 spreadsheet, $0.00800$ and $0.09504$, each divided by their sum $0.10304$: $D^+$ at $0.0776$ and $D^-$ at $0.9224$. Multiplying each by its test 2 conditional gives
\begin{equation*}
  0.0776 \times 0.80 = 0.0621, \qquad 0.9224 \times 0.096 = 0.0886, \qquad \frac{0.0621}{0.0621 + 0.0886} = 41.2\%,
\end{equation*}
as before. The full spreadsheet is the audit version; the pruned spreadsheet is the working version.

\paragraph{When the tests disagree.} Suppose $T_1$ positive and $T_2$ negative, still under independence. Rows 2 and 6:
\begin{equation*}
  \Pr(D^+ \mid T_1^+ \text{ and } T_2^-) = \frac{0.00160}{0.00160 + 0.08592} \approx 1.8\%.
\end{equation*}
The posterior is back near the prior of $1\%$. In odds: the positive multiplies the prior odds by $\mathrm{LR}^+ \approx 8.33$, the negative by $\mathrm{LR}^- \approx 0.221$, and the product $8.33 \times 0.221 = 1.84$ barely moves the odds: $0.0101 \times 1.84 = 0.0186$ in odds, which is $0.0186 / 1.0186 = 1.8\%$ as a probability. Any other combination of outcomes is one filter on the same table.

\begin{align*}
  \Pr(T_2^+ \mid T_1^+, D^+)      &= 0.90, & \Pr(T_2^+ \mid T_1^-, D^+)      &= 0.40, \\

\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
  0.80 \times 0.90 + 0.20 \times 0.40 &= 0.80 \text{ exactly}, \\
  0.096 \times 0.50 + 0.904 \times 0.053 &= 0.0959.

Without a reference standard they are statistical estimates (latent-class models with conditional dependence). A test's package insert does not carry them. \begin{align*}
  \Pr(D^+, T_1^+, T_2^+)      &= 0.01 \times 0.80 \times 0.90 = 0.00720, \\

\begin{equation*}
  \Pr(D^+ \mid T_1^+ \text{ and } T_2^+) = \frac{0.00720}{0.00720 + 0.04752} \approx 13.2\%.
\end{equation*}
In odds: the likelihood ratio of the second positive given the first is $0.90 / 0.50 = 1.8$ against the marginal $8.33$, so
\begin{equation*}
  0.0842 \times 1.8 = 0.152, \qquad \frac{0.152}{1 + 0.152} = 13.2\%.
\end{equation*}
Confirmation should therefore use a test whose error sources are independent of the screen's and whose specificity is high. A different analyte or mechanism gives the independence; the specificity is the second test's own. Repeating the same test is informative only where its error is random rather than intrinsic to the patient.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Why the misreading persists}

One cause of the misreading, a relative figure taken for an absolute one, is visible in the notations themselves. ARR and RRR reach the reader as summary statistics without the table that produced them, and a reader given ``$95\%$ relative risk reduction'' has no way back to the absolute scale. The RRR discards that scale. The $2\times 2$ table and the tree keep it in the counts; Bayes' formula shows it only after substitution. Kahwati et al.\ (2017) \cite{kahwati2017}, reviewing studies of prescribers, found that the same result presented as a relative rather than an absolute reduction raises the perceived effectiveness of a treatment and the willingness to prescribe it.

Brown (2021) \cite{brown2021} and Olliaro et al.\ (2021) \cite{olliaro2021} recompute the COVID-19 mRNA vaccine trials both ways. The trial publications reported the case counts; what reached the public were the relative reductions of $94$--$95\%$. The absolute reductions over the trial follow-up of about two months were $0.7$--$1.2\%$, depending on the denominator, with numbers needed to vaccinate from $81$ to $142$ as the two papers compute them from the unrounded rates. Both figures are correct and answer different questions: the relative figure transports to other attack rates, the absolute figure belongs to two months at the trial's incidence and grows with exposure.

\subsection{Recommendations}

For reporting, the recommendation is the counts. Primary reports are held to this by STARD 2015, item 23 \cite{bossuyt2015}, and CONSORT 2010, item 17b \cite{schulz2010}; the counts drop out in abstracts, press releases, advertising, and public statements. The recommendation is that the $2\times 2$ table of absolute counts travel with every derived statistic (PPV, ARR, RRR, sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratio) into those channels. A tree of four leaves adds the conditional structure and takes a quarter of a page.

For training, the recommendation is all four notations. Epidemiology and clinical curricula today introduce conditional probability through the contingency table and its derived statistics. The table is complete and concise, and it makes one thing salient, the joint frequency of each cell. Each of the other notations makes one other thing salient. The tree shows the flow from prior to posterior; the formula, the algebraic identity; the odds form, the update as a multiplication. The absolute scale is visible wherever the counts are, in the tree and in the table. The natural-frequency studies \cite{gigerenzer1995, hoffrage1998, binder2015, binder2018, binder2021, kunzelmann2022} put the gain from the counts at 30 percentage points in the first comparison and about 20 pooled across studies \cite{mcdowell2017}. Medical, epidemiology, and public-health curricula and prescribers' continuing education should cover every notation of Section~\ref{sec:four} through the mapping of Section~\ref{sec:iso} and the procedure of Section~\ref{sec:proc}. The cost is a few hours in the existing biostatistics block.

\subsection{The protocol is a column}

Section~\ref{sec:sheet} gave a two-row spreadsheet in which the posterior of one step is the prior of the next. That is Bayes' rule applied once per test; drawn as arrows from the disease to each test, it is the simplest Bayesian network \cite{pearl1988}, written out one row at a time. The protocol of this section's title is the process that produced an observation, and the claim is that it is a column of that spreadsheet.

The same spreadsheet also settles a class of puzzles that look like disputes about probability. In the Monty Hall problem a contestant picks one of three doors, the host opens another that hides a goat, and the question is whether to switch. In the two-children problem of Gardner \cite{gardner1959}, analysed by Bar-Hillel and Falk \cite{barhillel1982}, a family with two children has at least one girl, and the question is the probability of two girls. In both, two careful readers see the same observation and reach two different numbers. They differ because the process that produced the observation is part of the evidence. A host who knows where the car is and opens an empty door has performed one event; a door that happens to swing open on an empty room is another event, with a different probability under each hypothesis. With the contestant at door 1 and door 3 opened, the column over the car behind door 1, 2, 3 reads $\tfrac{1}{2}, 1, 0$ under a host who knows and $\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, 0$ under a door opened at random that happened to show a goat; the probability that switching wins is $\tfrac{2}{3}$ in the first case and $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in the second. In the spreadsheet that process occupies one column: the column that holds the probability of the observation under each state of the world. The two readers fill that column with different values, and the table then gives each of them a different posterior.

The correlated case of Section~\ref{sec:sheet} is the diagnostic instance. Two positive results give $41.2\%$ when the tests are independent (Table~\ref{tab:s2}) and $13.2\%$ when the second test repeats half of the first test's false positives (Table~\ref{tab:corr}). Nothing in the two results decides between those figures; the column that decides is the one that says how the second result was produced. Once the columns are filled, the table fixes the number; with the rows fixed by the statement of the problem, a dispute about the number is a dispute about a column. When the source does not state a column, the number is undetermined, in the same way that a missing base rate leaves step 1 of the procedure undetermined.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
  \item \emph{Binary outcomes.} The examples assume binary disease status and binary test results. The mapping extends to graded severity, competing diagnoses, and ordinal scores, with correspondingly larger tables, trees, and spreadsheets; the paper does not work that out.
  \item \emph{Step 5 is untested.} The three checks have not been evaluated on students or clinicians.
  \item \emph{Scope of the cited evidence.} Casscells et al.\ and Kahwati et al.\ document misreading by individual clinicians; Brown and Olliaro et al.\ document reporting in specific trials. That these patterns carry into regulatory and public communication is consistent with the evidence and not established by it.
\end{itemize}
