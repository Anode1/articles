\begin{abstract}
Automatic layout tools place the boxes of a diagram by minimising a weighted sum of
aesthetic criteria: few edge crossings, no overlapping boxes, edges of similar length. If
people drew the same way, a diagram laid out by hand would be a local minimum of that sum
and a tool started from it would find nothing to change. We test each criterion separately
on 1{,}571 diagrams whose coordinates a person chose: 571 WikiPathways biological pathways,
701 Reactome SBGN maps and 299 BPMN process models, three communities with different
authoring tools. The test is direct: move each box a small distance in each of sixteen
directions and ask whether any move lowers the criterion. Under crossings and overlap
together, every box in a hand layout is held in two corpora and 94\% are in the third
\pilot: the hand layout is a local minimum. Under uniform edge length, the criterion that
carries 94\% of the energy as tools weight it, no box is held\pilot. A stress layout of the
same graphs by \texttt{neato} has the opposite profile: held under edge length, free under
crossings and overlap. Fitting the weights by inverse optimisation returns weight zero for
edge length on every corpus, which this explains: any positive weight on it makes the hand
layout non-stationary. Of three further criteria, alignment of boxes into shared rows and
columns holds half the boxes in pathways and three quarters in process models\pilot{}
and almost none in the tool's layouts; orthogonality of edges holds fewer; separation of
boxes from edges holds none. The diagrams people draw are near-trees (median edges per box
1.06, 0.96 and 1.03, against 1.30 for the Rome benchmark), so crossings, the criterion the
field's theory is built on, have almost nothing to remove. A tool builder has two changes
to make: stop spending the energy on edge length, and add alignment. The measurement runs
on a small C library for stochastic search whose distinctive feature is a matched-budget
random control, the latest of a series of tools by the author that report when their own
answer should not be trusted.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A diagram is a graph with a drawing: boxes for the nodes, lines for the edges, and a
position for every box. When a program chooses the positions it minimises an
\emph{energy}, a weighted sum of quantities the field has agreed are bad: edges that cross,
boxes that overlap, edges of very unequal length, boxes that sit on edges they do not belong
to \cite{tdb88, dh96, purchase02, kobourov}. The weights are asserted by the tool's author.
The premise is that a person drawing by hand is doing the same thing more slowly, so that
the drawing they accept is one a tool would also accept.

The premise has a direct test, one criterion at a time. Take a diagram a person drew. Move
one box a small distance in each of several directions and ask whether any of the moves
lowers the criterion. If none does, the box is \emph{held}: the person has put it where the
criterion wants it. Do this for every box and the fraction held says how much of the layout
is a local minimum of that criterion. A criterion that holds every box is one the person
satisfied; one that holds no box is one the person did not consider, or traded away.

We ran the test on 1{,}571 diagrams from three unrelated communities
(Section~\ref{sec:corpora}) under a pre-registered analysis
(\texttt{PREREGISTRATION-STATIONARITY.md} in the repository). The findings, with the
exploratory numbers:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Under crossings and overlap together the hand layout is a local minimum: the
  fraction of boxes held is 1.00, 1.00 and 0.94\pilot{} on the three corpora. Under uniform
  edge length it is not: 0.00, 0.06 and 0.10\pilot. Under the three criteria summed with the
  usual equal weights the length term dominates and 0.03 to 0.10\pilot{} are held, which is
  what an earlier version of this study, using a capped descent rather than the directional
  test, reported as ``hand layouts are not stationary''. They are not stationary in edge
  length, and in nothing else among the base criteria.
\item A stress layout of the same graphs with the same box sizes (\texttt{neato}) is the
  mirror image: held under edge length on 0.26 to 0.47\pilot{} of boxes, held under
  crossings and overlap on only 0.51 to 0.84\pilot.
\item Fitting the energy's weights by inverse optimisation gives uniform edge length the
  weight 0.00 on every corpus. Finding 1 says why: any positive weight on a criterion that
  holds no box makes the sum hold no box.
\item Of three candidate criteria not in the base energy, alignment of boxes into shared
  rows and columns holds 0.53, 0.28 and 0.79\pilot{} of boxes in hand layouts and under
  0.06 in \texttt{neato}'s; orthogonality of edges holds 0.19, 0.13 and 0.45\pilot;
  separation of boxes from edges they do not join holds none. In WikiPathways a third of
  boxes share an exact coordinate with another box while only 2\% of coordinates are
  integers, so the alignment is the person's act and not the editor's grid; in BPMN it is
  partly the grid and mostly the lanes.
\item The diagrams people draw are near-trees: median edges per box 1.06, 0.96 and 1.03,
  against 1.30 in the Rome benchmark the field tests on \cite{rome}. Crossings are what the
  theory is about \cite{gj83}; on the diagrams people actually draw there are almost none
  to remove, which is part of why finding 1 holds.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of the paper is the foundation behind the test (Section~\ref{sec:foundations}),
the instrument (Section~\ref{sec:instrument}), the corpora, the method, the measurements,
the alternatives not yet excluded (Section~\ref{sec:alternatives}), and what is already
known (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

\section{Foundations}\label{sec:foundations}

\paragraph{The energy.} A layout of a graph with $N$ nodes is a point
$x \in \mathbb{R}^{2N}$, two coordinates per node, the boxes' sizes held fixed. An aesthetic
energy is
\begin{equation}
E_w(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k\, T_k(x), \qquad w_k \ge 0,
\end{equation}
where each term $T_k$ is a quantity to be made small. The tool's author asserts the
$w_k$; the field of multicriteria layout is the choice of the $T_k$ and the $w_k$
\cite{dh96, purchase02}.

\paragraph{Local minimum.} A point $x$ is a local minimum of $E$ if there is a radius
$r > 0$ with $E(y) \ge E(x)$ for every $y$ within distance $r$ of $x$. For a differentiable
$E$ this implies $\nabla E(x) = 0$. Two of our terms are not differentiable: crossings is a
count, piecewise constant in $x$, and overlap has corners where boxes touch. So the test
uses no gradient. Fix a radius $d$ and a set $V$ of unit directions in the plane (sixteen,
equally spaced). For node $i$ and direction $v$ let $x + d\,v^{(i)}$ be the layout with
node $i$ moved by $d$ along $v$ and every other node fixed. Node $i$ is \emph{held} at
radius $d$ if
\begin{equation}
E(x + d\,v^{(i)}) \ge E(x) \quad \text{for every } v \in V,
\label{eq:held}
\end{equation}
and the estimand is
\begin{equation}
q_d(x) = \frac{1}{N}\,\#\{\,i : \text{node } i \text{ is held at radius } d\,\}.
\label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
$q_d = 1$ says the layout is a local minimum of $E$ over single-node moves of length $d$;
for smooth $E$ and small $d$ it says $\nabla E(x) = 0$ to within the resolution of $V$.
Because~\eqref{eq:held} is a comparison of two energies, it is defined for every term, and
because it is evaluated per term it separates the criteria a person satisfied from those
they did not. A descent-based measure (how far a capped descent moves the layout, the
estimand of the first version of this study) answers the same question for the sum but
cannot separate the terms; it is retained as a secondary.

\paragraph{The inverse problem.} Given layouts $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(G)}$ that people
accepted, inverse optimisation asks for the weights $w$ under which they are nearest to
stationary \cite{keshavarz11, chan19}. With the directional differences
$D_{iv} T_k = T_k(x + d\,v^{(i)}) - T_k(x)$ in hand, node $i$ is held under $w$ exactly
when $\sum_k w_k D_{iv} T_k \ge 0$ for all $v$, a set of linear inequalities in $w$. The
residual is the smallest total violation over $w$ on the simplex, a linear program, and it
is zero only if some weighting of the terms makes the layout a local minimum. A term with
$D_{iv} T_k < 0$ for some $v$ at almost every node (uniform edge length, at any layout
whose edges are not all the same length) cannot take positive weight without violating the
inequalities at those nodes, which is the zero weight the random-search fit found.

\paragraph{The null and the control.} A test that can only succeed is not a test. The
\emph{stationarity reference}: $q_d$ on a layout a descent has converged on must be near
one, and it is (0.94 to 1.00\pilot{} on every corpus and energy). The \emph{tool control}:
$q_d$ on a layout produced by a real tool from the same graph, which says what a layout
that does minimise something scores on each term. The \emph{matched-budget control}:
uniform random placement at the same number of energy evaluations, which the hand layout
must beat, or ``held under crossings'' would be a property of sparse graphs and not of the
person.

\section{The instrument}\label{sec:instrument}

The descents, the control and the paired statistics come from \texttt{cjitter}, a C library
of four stochastic searches over a box of real variables: hill climbing with restarts,
simulated annealing, a genetic algorithm, and uniform sampling as the control. Every run is
byte-reproducible from its seed on every platform, every method constant is a visible
field, and \texttt{cjitter\_compare} runs all four at the same budget on the same seeds and
reports an exact paired sign test, Holm-corrected, against the control. ``Better than
uniform sampling at equal cost'' is the only claim it lets a search make.

It is the latest of a series of small tools by the author, each of which reports the
condition under which its own answer is wrong:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
year & tool & what it reports against itself \\
\midrule
1997 & backpropagation thesis \cite{bpfnn97} & descent on an error energy, derived \\
2001 & label placer & per-label random steps, kept if the summed overlap fell \\
2011 & least squares, now \texttt{linearr} & when a line is the wrong shape \\
2025 & \texttt{bpnn} & when the network has memorised the rows \\
2026 & \texttt{cjitter} & when a search does no better than sampling \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The 1997 thesis derives backpropagation as gradient descent on an error surface; the 2001
placer, deployed in industry, is the same descent without a gradient, one label moved a
random step and the step kept when the total cost fell. That operator, a proposal that moves
one object and is judged on the summed energy, is the \texttt{block} field of the library,
and the directional test of equation~\eqref{eq:held} is the same proposal made
exhaustive: every direction tried, none accepted, the answer read off. The present paper
turns the instrument around: instead of descending an energy to place objects, it starts
from where a person placed them and reads which terms pull.

The layouts are working documents, not aesthetic submissions: Reactome diagrams are laid

bounding box fits the unit square, box sizes scaled with it. The pre-registered run
restricts to 15 to 40 nodes; the pilot used 10 to 400. Duplicate pathways (ortholog copies
of one human pathway) are removed by model identity. \pending{counts after deduplication;

an exact $y$ 0.28, 0.12 and 0.73. WikiPathways authors align by hand on a continuous
canvas; BPMN authors work on a grid inside lanes; Reactome's curation tool rounds but its
authors align little.

\section{The method}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Terms.} With $n$ nodes, $m$ edges, box $i$ of width $w_i$ and height $h_i$, $L$
the median edge length of the layout under test:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
term & definition & zero when \\
\midrule
$T_C$ crossings & pairs of edges with no shared endpoint that cross, $/m$ & planar drawing \\
$T_O$ overlap & total overlap area of boxes $/$ total box area & no two boxes overlap \\
$T_L$ length uniformity & mean over edges of $((\ell - L)/L)^2$ & all edges of length $L$ \\
$T_R$ orthogonality & mean over edges of $\min(|\Delta x|,|\Delta y|)/(|\Delta x|+|\Delta y|)$ & every edge axis-aligned \\
$T_A$ alignment & mean over nodes of $1/(1 + \sum_{j \ne i} e^{-(\Delta x/s)^2} + e^{-(\Delta y/s)^2})$, $s = 0.02$ & every node shares a row and a column \\
$T_N$ node--edge & penalty for a node within $(w+h)/4$ of an edge it does not join & no node sits on a foreign edge \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The base energy is $T_C + T_O + T_L$ with weights $(1,1,1)$, the form of the
simulated-annealing layout of Davidson and Harel \cite{dh96} and of the multicriteria
tradition since \cite{ahmed20}. The field's other default is stress \cite{kamada89,
gansner05}, $\sum_{i<j} (\|x_i - x_j\| - L\,\delta_{ij})^2 / \delta_{ij}^2$ with
$\delta_{ij}$ the graph distance, which force-directed tools and \texttt{neato} minimise
and which Dwyer et al.\ \cite{dwyer09} found human choices track; it is the second base
energy of the pre-registered run. \pending{stress row in every table} $T_R$, $T_A$ and
$T_N$ are the candidates for what the base energy misses. The alignment definition above
is the kernel form A3; two simpler forms, A1 (distance to the nearest node sharing a row
or column) and A2 (rows and columns priced separately), are reported as sensitivities,
together with HOLA's gridiness \cite{hola16}, the fraction of nodes in some alignment of
three or more, which is the published form. A1 has a corner at exact alignment and A3 does
not, and the corner is what holds a box: A1 holds 0.53 of WikiPathways boxes and A3 holds
0.25\pilot. Which form is primary is declared before the run.

All terms are evaluated here on straight centre-to-centre segments. GPML and SBGN store
waypoints and BPMN routes edges orthogonally, so crossings, orthogonality and node--edge
separation are measured on a drawing the person did not see. \pending{the same terms on
the stored polylines, with the fraction of edges that have waypoints per corpus}

\paragraph{Directional test.} Radius $d \in \{0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05\}$ of the drawing
width, sixteen directions, every node, each term alone and the declared sums. Primary cell
$d = 0.02$. Deterministic: no seed, no budget.

\paragraph{Descent, secondary.} Hill climbing, one node per proposal, initial step set
from the cap, each node clipped to a disc of radius $d$ around its start, 4000 energy
evaluations, 15 seeds per instance; reported as the fraction of the cap used and the
fraction of energy removed. The exploratory runs used a step of $0.1$, five times the cap,
so that nearly every proposal landed on the cap circle; the base-energy result did not
depend on it (98.5 to 95.9\% of the cap used as the step fell from 0.1 to 0.005) and the
alignment result did (47 to 18\%), which is why the directional test is primary.

\paragraph{Fitting.} Weights on the simplex, the linear program of
Section~\ref{sec:foundations} on a training half of each corpus, residual reported on the
held-out half; the random search of the pilot (200 log-uniform draws \cite{bergstra12})
beside it as a check. One candidate term added to the base at a time, then all six.

\paragraph{Statistics.} The unit of inference is the diagram. Per corpus, the paired
comparison of $q_d$ between two energies, or between the hand layout and the tool control,
is the exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test with the exact sign test beside it, Hodges--Lehmann
shift and distribution-free interval; Holm correction over the declared family within each
corpus. Equivalence claims (``no reweighting repairs it'') are two one-sided tests against
a declared margin, never a failure to reject. \pending{the families, as declared in the
signed pre-registration}

\section{What else could produce this}\label{sec:alternatives}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The editor snapped to a grid}, so alignment is the tool's. WikiPathways has
  2\% integer coordinates and a third of boxes exactly aligned, so there it is the
  person's. BPMN is snapped. Test, declared: jitter every box by half a grid cell and
  repeat; the alignment that survives is the person's.
\item \textbf{Straight segments where the person drew polylines.} GPML and SBGN store
  waypoints and BPMN routes edges orthogonally; crossings, orthogonality and node--edge
  separation are here evaluated on centre-to-centre segments the person never saw. Test:
  the same table on the drawn polylines. \pending{}
\item \textbf{The person minimised a constrained energy whose constraints are absent}:
  reading direction, layers, compartments, lanes. The BPMN $y$-alignment of 0.73 is the
  lanes. Test: a flow term (penalty on edges running against the reading direction) and
  containment as a hard constraint, added as candidates. \pending{}
\item \textbf{The radius.} At $d = 0.02$ on a thousand-pixel canvas the move is twenty
  pixels, about one grid step; a person cannot be stationary below the resolution they
  control. Test: the table at every cap, and at $d$ in units of box width and of median
  edge length.
\item \textbf{Selection.} Near-trees make crossings easy. Test: $q_d$ against $m/n$ within
  each corpus.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Aesthetic criteria and their weighting go back to Tamassia, Di Battista and Batini
\cite{tdb88} and were made an explicit energy by Davidson and Harel \cite{dh96}; Purchase
\cite{purchase02} gave the criteria their metrics, and that list has no alignment metric.
Whether people agree with the criteria has been asked three ways. By having people draw:
van Ham and Rogowitz \cite{vanham08} found hand layouts with fewer crossings than the
force-directed algorithm produced; Dwyer et al.\ \cite{dwyer09} had 32 participants lay
out one graph and 194 judges rank the results against three automatic layouts, and found
the judges' choices track stress; Purchase, Pilcher and Plimmer \cite{purchase12} had
participants draw from adjacency lists and found crossing removal the strongest aesthetic
and alignment to a grid important; Kieffer et al.\ \cite{hola16} collected 17 designers'
layouts of 8 small graphs, ranked by 66 judges, and found their gridiness, the fraction of
nodes in some alignment of three or more, correlated with rank on all 8 graphs, which is
where HOLA's alignment step comes from. Alignment as a human criterion is therefore known
since 2012; what is new here is the count of how many hand-placed boxes it holds, on
diagrams of 15 to 40 boxes, beside the counts for the criteria tools weight. By showing
people drawings: Purchase et al.\ \cite{purchase20} ran a Turing test in which hand
drawings were distinguishable from algorithmic ones and judged better; Klammler,
Mchedlidze and Pak \cite{klammler19} fitted the weights of a linear aesthetic combination
by Nelder--Mead to discriminate good from worsened layouts, without human coordinates, and
wrote that human-labelled data ``may become a critically important accomplishment in the
graph drawing field''. By learning from examples: Masui \cite{masui94} learned a weighted
constraint combination from user-supplied good and bad layouts by genetic programming,
and Sp\"onemann, Duderstadt and von Hanxleden \cite{spoenemann14} adjusted weights from
user selections among candidates; neither tested whether the examples were minima of the
learned energy. The closest prior descent from a hand layout is Flud \cite{flud22}:
simulated annealing continued from crowd layouts of pathway diagrams raised an asserted
five-term score, on three networks, read as a success of the hybrid; the cap, the
per-term test, the reference and the controls here are what turn that observation into a
measurement of the score. Van Wageningen, Mchedlidze and Telea \cite{wageningen25} show
drawings with identical metric values that differ in quality, which is why a fitted
energy with no residual would still not be the energy people use, and why the claims here
stay on the negative side. Kobourov, Pupyrev and Saket \cite{kobourov14} found crossings
matter little on sparse large graphs, consistent with finding 5. Inverse optimisation, the
recovery of an objective from solutions taken as optimal, is due to Ahuja and Orlin
\cite{ahuja01} for linear programs and to Keshavarz, Wang and Boyd \cite{keshavarz11} for
the convex case; Chan, Lee and Terekhov \cite{chan19} define the goodness-of-fit residual
reported here. Matched-budget random controls are standard in hyperparameter search
\cite{bergstra12} and were prescribed as the baseline for genetic algorithms in 1995
\cite{jw95}; we have not found them in layout. No paper fitting energy weights to
human-placed coordinates was found in four searches of 2024 to 2026 work; the nearest,
Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang26}, learns from 64{,}222 pairwise preference labels, not
coordinates.

\section{Conclusion}

People draw at a local minimum of the criteria that are either satisfied or not: no box on
another, no crossing a small move removes. They draw nowhere near a minimum of uniform
edge length, the smooth criterion that carries most of the energy in the tools, and a
tool's own layouts show the opposite profile. What people do that the base energy does not
price is align boxes into rows and columns, by hand where the canvas is continuous. A tool
builder reading this has two changes to make and one number to report: take the weight off
edge length, add alignment, and print the fraction of boxes the energy holds when started
from a layout a person accepted.
